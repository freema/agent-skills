# Handling objections

The protocol, in order:

1. **Listen actively** — let them finish; nod; don't prepare the reply while they talk.
2. **Appreciate** — "Good point, thanks for raising it."
3. **Check understanding** — "If I understand you right, the concern is …?"
4. **Reformulate positively** — turn the attack into the underlying interest.
5. **Hand it back** with an open question — *How…?* or *What…?* — never *Why…?*
   ("Why do you think that?" sounds like an accusation; "What would need to be
   true for this to work for you?" sounds like collaboration.)

Decide first whether the question is **logical** (answer with a fact or a
mechanism) or **emotional** (acknowledge first, facts second).

## Typical attacks and reformulations

| Attack | Underlying interest | Reformulation + hand-back |
|---|---|---|
| "Your proposal is impractical and will only cause trouble." | Wants low friction | "So the key for you is that it must not add work for the team. What would 'practical' look like day to day?" |
| "It is too expensive, we have no budget." | Wants value per cost | "You want to be sure the return justifies the spend. What return would make it worth it?" |
| "It doesn't meet all our requirements." | Wants completeness | "You need certainty the important cases are covered. Which requirement matters most?" |
| "I don't believe you can launch this on schedule." | Wants predictability | "Delivery risk is the concern. What milestones would give you confidence?" |
| "I don't understand what you are proposing." | Wants clarity | "Let me try it differently. What part is least clear — the problem or the solution?" |
| "Your solution didn't meet my expectations." | Wants to be heard | "Something you expected didn't happen. What did you expect to see?" |
| "We already tried this and it didn't work." | Wants to avoid repeating pain | "You've seen this fail before. What was different then — and what would have to differ now?" |
| "Your proposal doesn't suit me." | Unspecified | "Help me understand what would suit you. What is missing?" |

## Rules of thumb

- Never argue the premise in the first sentence. Acknowledge first.
- One reply, then hand back. Don't stack three arguments.
- If you don't know: say so, say when you will know, move on.
- Agree upfront when questions are taken; then keep that agreement.
